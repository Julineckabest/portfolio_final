VR Parenting Simulator

To what extent can a simplified VR parenting simulation — with deliberately constrained interaction design — achieve the perception of naturalness required for genuine empathy-building? This was the research question that shaped every design decision in this project.

Project Overview

Empathy is difficult to design for. This project explored whether VR — with the right interaction design — could create conditions for genuine emotional connection rather than just simulated presence. Built with Unity XR for Oculus Quest 2, the prototype tested a specific hypothesis: that naturalistic, low-complexity interactions with a responsive baby avatar could generate real empathy-building moments, not just novelty.

Role

Sole Designer & Developer

Duration

6 months (Dec 2025 - May 2026)

Project Deliverables

VR prototype, design documentation, research runnings, and playtesting reports, analysis of user feedback & conclusions, and final presentation materials.

Design Journey

01

Empathise & Define

The starting point was a genuine question: what does parenting feel like, and can VR simulate enough of that to shift perspective? I grounded the project in research on empathy design, the limits of the uncanny valley in character design, and what "naturalness" actually means as a metric in immersive experience research.

Scoping was as important as the research question itself — a simplified prototype forces you to decide what is essential to the experience. I defined the core: responsive baby avatar, hand-tracked physical interactions, emotionally coherent environment.

02

Ideate & Prototype

The interaction system needed to feel parental — not just functional. I designed grab, hold, and comfort interactions that mapped physically to real caregiving gestures. The key design question for the UI was: how little can you show without the player feeling lost? I kept the interface invisible — no HUD, no prompts beyond the environment itself.

The baby avatar was created in Blender using MakeHuman add-ons with a full rig and facial expressions. Getting the expressiveness right was critical — the avatar needed to respond in ways that triggered real emotional reactions without sliding into uncanny territory. Multiple iterations of blendshapes and animation timing tested that balance.

03

Environment as Emotional Design

The room wasn't just a backdrop — it was designed to evoke a specific emotional register. Warm, domestic, slightly worn. The spatial design needed to communicate that a real person lives here, because that specificity is what makes empathy possible. A generic nursery produces generic feelings.

Third-party assets from the Unity Asset Store were scaled to an average adult height, keeping the physical proportions intuitive for players in the headset — a detail that matters more than it sounds when your hands are tracked and the scale feels wrong.

04

Interaction & Atmosphere

The interactions were designed around emotional coherence — each one had to feel connected to the experience of caring for someone. Players could pick up, hold, and soothe the baby, with haptic feedback calibrated to reinforce the physicality. The design also introduced ethical framing: what happens when you put down the baby? What choices does the space allow, and which does it resist? Those edges were intentional.

The soundscape — ambient room noise, infant sounds, gentle music — was carefully layered to support presence. Sound does emotional work that visuals can't, especially in VR where attention is distributed across the whole environment.

05

Test & Analyse

Ten playtesting sessions were run across deliberately diverse participant groups: people new to VR, experienced VR users, parents and caregivers, and individuals with no caregiving background. That range was important — I wasn't just testing usability, I was testing whether the empathy effect was consistent or contingent on prior experience.

Data collection combined quantitative measures (presence scales, task completion) with qualitative post-session interviews. Findings were analysed against naturalistic design principles, and iterated into the prototype. The Miro board became the thinking space — mapping what worked, what broke presence, and what the data couldn't fully explain.

Research & Implementation

Interaction Design Decisions

Custom interaction framework built on Unity XR Interaction Toolkit. The key design decision was restraint — limiting interactions to grab, hold, and push, rather than complex gesture sets. Fewer interactions, more presence.

Tools: C#, XR Toolkit, Oculus Integration SDK

Performance as UX

Maintaining 72fps on Quest 2 wasn't a technical goal alone — frame drops break presence, and presence is the mechanism through which empathy works in VR. LOD systems, occlusion culling, texture atlasing, and baked lighting were optimisation tools in service of the user experience.

Techniques: URP optimisation, mesh combining, shader LOD

Emotional Feedback Systems

Dissolve shaders using noise textures created the memory-fragmentation visual effect — a design decision rooted in the research question about perception of reality. Scriptable object-based room configuration kept the environment authorable and testable across iterations.

Tools: Shader Graph, ScriptableObjects, Unity Events

Impact & Conclusion

The findings confirmed what the research question was probing: even a simplified, constrained VR simulation can generate naturalistic perception and genuine emotional response — but only when the interaction design prioritises presence over feature richness. Playtesters reported strong emotional connection to the avatar. Several participants across the non-parent group described experiencing unfamiliar feelings of protectiveness. That was the goal. The project demonstrated that empathy design in VR is fundamentally an interaction design problem, not a graphics fidelity problem.
