Dottyvile 3D Environment

How do you design a space that feels historically true without becoming a lecture? This team project asked exactly that — recreating a WWI-era hospital ward at Craiglockhart as an interactive experience where players discover history through exploration, not exposition.

Environment Design 3D Modelling Interaction Design Team Project

The Experience

The Craiglockhart campus in Edinburgh holds a layered history — a convent, a hydropathic hotel, and during WWI, a war hospital where poets Wilfred Owen and Siegfried Sassoon were treated. The goal was to recreate a section of that hospital (known as Dottyville) as an explorable 3D environment that could serve both the existing War Poets exhibition and anyone curious about that period.

The experience puts you in the role of a hospital worker collecting patient writings for the Hydra — the real literary magazine the hospital ran as part of occupational therapy. Narrative is embedded in objects. You don't read a history essay; you walk through one.

My Role

This was a collaborative team project. My responsibilities included historical and audience research, 3D modelling of period-accurate interior assets in Blender, and designing and implementing the menu system (start screen and pause menu with resume logic).

Key contributions:

  • Historical research and audience definition
  • 3D modelling of interior assets for the WWI era in Blender
  • Menu system design and C# implementation (start/pause/resume)
  • Client presentation and project documentation

Design Process

01 — Research & Audience

I started by researching the historical context of Craiglockhart — the War Poets Collection at Napier University, firsthand accounts from patients, and the therapeutic role the Hydra magazine played. Understanding the context wasn't just background research; it directly shaped every design decision, from which objects to model to how information would be revealed.

The target audience includes people interested in WWI history and poetry, Napier students, and visitors to the Craiglockhart campus. The key insight from research: this audience responds to atmosphere and discovery — they want to feel present in a moment, not guided through a timeline. That meant environmental storytelling, not text panels.

02 — Narrative Architecture

The narrative premise is deliberately minimal: you work at the hospital and collect patient writings for the Hydra magazine. There's no complex story — the environment carries the meaning. Players move through the space at their own pace, and the act of collecting writings mirrors the real therapeutic practice at the hospital.

This non-linear, exploratory structure was a deliberate design choice. Prescriptive guided tours would undermine the sense of discovery that makes historical spaces feel alive. The design trusts the player to be curious.

03 — Interaction & UI Design

The interface was designed to disappear into the experience. A minimal HUD keeps the player's attention on the environment, not a dashboard. I made deliberate decisions about what feedback the player needs and what would break immersion.

  • Crosshair changes to red over interactive objects — clear affordance without breaking atmosphere
  • Writings glow yellow/green on hover, then open on click — progressive disclosure keeps curiosity active
  • Escape key pauses and reveals a clean menu with resume, options (volume), and main menu — low cognitive load
  • Collection popups confirm progress without interrupting movement
  • Spatial audio cues reward exploration and deepen immersion

04 — Development

Built in Unity 2023.2.20f1 using the Universal Render Pipeline (URP). The deliberate choice to avoid third-party plugins meant every system was purposefully understood and controllable — important for a team where responsibilities were divided.

  • Unity URP with C# scripting
  • TextMeshPro for all in-world text interactions
  • Rigidbody physics for object interaction
  • Custom-mixed spatial audio
  • Deployed as standalone .exe and walkthrough video for the client

04.1 — My Contribution: Menu System

The menu was my specific design and development ownership. First impressions matter — the start screen sets the tone before the player enters the space, so the visual language needed to feel historically grounded without being heavy-handed. The pause menu needed to be discoverable without interrupting flow; the escape key is intuitive but not always obvious to non-gamers.

  • UI layout and visual design for start and pause states
  • C# scripting for pause/resume logic and state management
  • Button interaction flows and navigation hierarchy
  • Volume control in options to accommodate different environments
  • Integration with the broader team's game systems

My 3D Models

Each asset was modelled in Blender with period accuracy in mind — hospital furniture, medical tools, and everyday objects from a WWI ward. Getting the scale and material feel right was important; these objects are what make the space feel inhabited rather than staged.

Outcome & Reflection

73.8%

Academic grade

The project was well received by academic staff and the client. Working within a team taught me to design for handoffs — my menu system and 3D models had to integrate cleanly with others' work. The constraint of period accuracy was genuinely useful: it forced me to research before I modelled, and every design decision had a reason beyond aesthetics.

If I were to revisit it, I'd invest more in spatial audio and lighting variation across rooms — those are the details that make historical spaces feel real rather than reconstructed.
