Homebound: Lost Signals

How much can space, atmosphere, and sound communicate without explicit UI? This was the central design question — building a post-apocalyptic first-person prototype where the environment does the storytelling, and every UI decision was weighed against immersion.

Development Process

The central design question was: how do you build tension and narrative coherence in 8 weeks, solo, on a student budget? Every decision came back to that constraint — and constraints turned out to be generative.

The world is set in an abandoned factory in the middle of a forest. I sourced post-apocalyptic assets from the FAB digital marketplace, then reshaped the terrain to feel inhabited rather than generic — adding a lake, varying ground textures, building a landscape the player could approach from outside before entering. That exterior-to-interior transition was an intentional design beat: it gives the player a moment to absorb the atmosphere before the space closes in around them.

Atmospheric design was the first real design challenge. The deserted colour palette — brown and orange tones — needed to feel desolate without looking muddy. I worked through post-process volumes, sky atmosphere settings, and directional light colour iteratively. The HDRI backdrop I tried early on actually degraded the mood (and tanked performance), so I cut it. That's a lesson I've kept: not every reference technique fits every project.

Performance forced another meaningful design decision. The simulation ran with significant lag and "Video Memory exhausted" warnings when I pushed quality settings to EPIC. Dropping to MEDIUM scalability resolved the issue — but affected asset fidelity. I had to consciously design around that constraint: where does quality matter most to the player experience, and where can it be sacrificed? I prioritised the interior lighting and close-up interaction areas over distant terrain detail.

Player communication was a design problem I enjoyed. The character navigates with WASD and interacts using E — a red sphere indicates grabbable objects at arm's length. That visual affordance needed to feel diegetic enough not to break the atmosphere, but clear enough that players never felt confused about what was interactive. Objects either trigger on-screen text, disappear, or play sound effects — all of which communicate the post-apocalyptic backstory without exposition dumps.

A Mixamo-animated character inside the building displays dialogue that gives players a narrative anchor — "finding them" — without over-explaining the situation. Suggesting mystery is a more powerful design tool than explaining it.

The second level (an underground cave) introduced a level-transition design problem I hadn't anticipated: how do you handle state when returning from a sub-level? I resolved it by creating a "Level BACK" — a third scene duplicating the first, with an updated inventory state and a restored lifebar, but no intro panel. The environment is identical, which keeps disorientation low. Not elegant, but intentional and functional.

Sound was sourced from Pixabay's free library and two AI-generated sources (ProducerAI for soundtrack, Descript for radio script narration). Everything was edited in REAPER — cutting, speeding up, layering — to create an audio design that felt cohesive rather than patched together.

Design Phases

01

Define & Concept

Established the design question: what kind of narrative can atmosphere carry? Built a pitch deck scoping the tone, world, and player experience before a single asset was placed.

02

Narrative Architecture

Designed the world's storytelling logic — backstory communicated through animated characters and triggered dialogue, not text walls. Defined what the player is trying to understand.

03

Prototype & Iterate

Built, broke, and rebuilt the interaction system and atmospheric lighting across multiple passes. Performance constraints forced clear priority decisions about where quality mattered most.

04

Reflect

What worked: atmospheric restraint and the exterior-to-interior transition. What I'd revisit: a more elegant state management solution for level transitions instead of a duplicate level.

What I Took Away

This was my first solo project in Unreal Engine 5, and the learning curve was steep — but the constraints were clarifying. Designing and building alone meant every decision was mine to justify. I learned that atmosphere is a UX tool, that performance constraints force honest prioritisation, and that sound does more emotional work than most interfaces get credit for. I'm proud of what I made in 8 weeks, and I know exactly what I'd do differently next time.
