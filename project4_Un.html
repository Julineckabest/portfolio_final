3D Virtual Exhibition: Under the Ocean

How do you design curiosity? This virtual exhibition set out to answer that — creating an interactive underwater space where discovery, not instruction, drives the visitor's experience. The design challenge was to make something that felt genuinely atmospheric rather than educational-brochure-in-3D.

The Design Brief

More than 80% of the ocean remains unexplored. That statistic sits at the heart of this project — because that uncertainty is exactly what makes ocean mythology so persistent. “Monsters of the Ocean” is a virtual 3D exhibition designed around a simple visitor experience question: what would it feel like to encounter those creatures in a space built to evoke the deep ocean, not just represent it?

The exhibition is structured around four points of interest, each revealing different layers — prehistoric creatures, famous cinematic scenes, interactive 3D models, and a character guide named Jimmy. The progression is gradual and curiosity-driven. Visitors don't receive information passively; each interactive trigger reveals the next layer, so engagement is earned rather than given. The low-intensity blue lighting and spatial audio design create an atmosphere before any content is shown — the space does emotional work before the visitor reads a single word.

Audience & Context

The subject matter — prehistoric creatures, sea mythology, and cinematic sea monsters — spans multiple interest groups: paleontology enthusiasts, movie fans, folklore readers, and simply the curious. Blending factual history with folklore was a deliberate design decision: creatures like the Kraken and Moby Dick exist in popular culture already, which means visitors arrive with emotional reference points. The exhibition builds on that prior knowledge rather than replacing it with a lecture.

Audience: curious and adventurous individuals and families with older children (12+). The age limit reflects a content decision — some scenes are intense — but the overall tone is designed to feel wondrous rather than frightening. Jimmy's voice is friendly, even when the subject matter is dark. That contrast is intentional: the guide should feel like company, not narration.

The interaction model is consistent throughout — clicking a touchable point triggers audio and animation, gradually revealing each station. That repetition is a UX choice, not a limitation: consistent patterns reduce cognitive friction so visitors can focus on the experience, not the mechanics of using it. Navigation is intentionally minimal to serve non-technical users without patronising others.

Inspiration & Influence

The subject was mainly inspired by the American 3D IMAX movie Sea Monsters: A Prehistoric Adventure, which was made in 2007 and about prehistoric marine reptiles. It has also been tie-in to the same video game. The idea of placing the audience deep into the ocean to experience reptiles from a short distance was the primary inspiration for the project. 3D animation can simulate complex phenomena, leaving visitors with lasting impressions and a more profound understanding (Immersive Studio, n.d.). The other inspiration is based on folklore stories such as Kraken (pictured on the title page Twenty Thousand Leagues Under The Sea by Julies Verne) and Moby dick (giant whale). Although the creatures have different visual looks depending on Regine of the folklore, the pictures of the monsters in the exhibition are taken from modern Hollywood movies. Additionally, the idea of the Karkinos (Carcinus) giant crab from Greek mythology, pictured in The Ocean Hunter game in 1998, was a suitable match to the theme. Another artistic influence comes from movies such as Pirates of the Caribbean, Dead Man's Chest (picturing life close to the ocean and its mystical creature), and Jurrasic World (picturing Mossasourus being so close to the audience that there is the feeling of danger).

Visitor Experience Flow

The onboarding is deliberate: the exhibition opens in darkness and ambience, with three objects that guide the player without text instructions. One shows direction, one signals interaction, one waits. When the player engages, all three animate out of the scene — the exhibition has begun. This approach respects the visitor's intelligence while being completely self-explanatory. It's zero-friction onboarding through environment, not tooltip.

The first corridor uses images and terrain textures to build atmosphere before revealing anything interactive. The second station introduces the cinematic material — movie clips from Pirates of the Caribbean, Jurassic World, Godzilla Minus One — contextualising the mythological creatures in the cultural language visitors already know. Each station is designed to accumulate emotional intensity, not just information.

The third station presents a rotating 3D monster crab model — the first fully three-dimensional, manipulable object in the experience. That escalation is paced intentionally: by this point the visitor is invested enough to appreciate it. The final station reveals Jimmy in person alongside the Basilosaurus sculpture. Attaching the guide voice to a visible character creates the personal connection that audio alone can't. The monster-aesthetic of Jimmy's design fits the theme while remaining readable as friendly — that visual character design decision carries a lot of the tone.

Technical Decisions

The interaction model is built entirely on a single pattern: click a touching point, trigger animation from position A to B and play audio. That consistency was a design decision as much as a technical one — it meant the mechanics never distracted from the experience, and debugging was straightforward. The Mixamo humanoid character is animated in a looped "talking" gesture, timed to Jimmy's AI-generated voice script.

Asset sourcing (Unity Asset Store, Polyhaven, OpenGameArt, Mixamo) was constrained by both budget and the lighting environment — dark blue underwater atmosphere means assets need to hold their readability in low light. Materials were adjusted to suit the colour scheme rather than used as-is. The gap between what I wanted and what the budget allowed was a real constraint, and working within it taught me how to make selective quality decisions rather than distribute mediocrity evenly.

Reflection

The exhibition achieves its central goal: visitors feel like they're exploring, not being lectured. The consistent interaction model works well — it removes friction so the atmosphere can do its job. The biggest constraint I'd address with more time and budget is interaction variety: the single-trigger-per-station pattern is clean but limits how deep the curiosity-building can go. Varied interactions across stations — some exploratory, some timed, some conditional — would create more genuine discovery rather than curated reveals.

What I'm most pleased with is the tone. Getting something that feels dark and wondrous rather than just spooky or educational was the harder design challenge, and the combination of Jimmy's voice, the paced lighting, and the music earns it.
