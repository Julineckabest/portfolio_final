Silent Disco App

How do you design for community and shared music experience when the noise is the problem? This project started with that question — inspired by a visit to Pohoda festival in Slovakia, where noise restrictions were fragmenting the crowd rather than bringing people together. The Silent Disco App reimagines that experience from a UX-first perspective.

Project Overview

The Silent Disco App responds to a real tension at music festivals: the growing demand for noise-conscious, ecologically minded events clashes with the social mechanics that make festivals work. Silent discos solve the sound problem but create a new UX challenge — how do you maintain the feeling of shared experience when everyone is in their own audio bubble? The design process focused on that question: what features, information hierarchy, and social mechanics restore the sense of community?

Role

Solo project - full UXdesign and development

Duration

3 months (Design, Feedback Gathering & Implementation)

Tools

Figma, Adobe Illustrator

Development Journey

01

Empathise & Define

Research began with the human context: the psychological and social effects of shared music experiences, and what's lost when that shared layer is removed by noise restrictions. I looked at existing festival apps, silent disco platforms, and event-going behaviour to understand where current solutions fall short.

The core insight: existing silent disco apps solve the audio logistics, but they design out the social glue — who's on which channel, where are my friends, what's happening right now? The problem wasn't the silence. It was the social isolation silence created.

Target audience: 16–35, all genders, digitally comfortable, festival-regulars who value atmosphere as much as performance.

02

Ideate & Prototype

The fastest way to test whether an idea solves the right problem is to put something physical in front of people. Before opening Figma, I used low-fidelity paper prototyping — not as a shortcut, but as a deliberate design choice. Card and paper force you to focus on interaction flow and user mental models rather than visual polish.

The prototype mapped the core journeys: joining a dance party, finding friends, discovering what's playing. Each screen was tested for navigational clarity: could someone find what they needed without being told what to do?

03

Test & Gather Feedback

The paper prototype was tested with target audience members — festival-goers aged 16–35 who use their phones socially at events. Sessions combined task-based observation with post-interview questions, plus a QR-code quantitative survey at the end.

Key findings: Navigation between the social and music features was confusing — users expected them integrated, not separated. The friend-location feature created the most excitement, which confirmed the social isolation insight from research. Visual hierarchy needed more contrast between active and passive states.

04

Analyse & Synthesise

Survey data and interview notes were synthesised to identify patterns across users. Two personas emerged clearly from the data: the social connector (who uses the app to maintain group cohesion) and the music explorer (who primarily wants to discover and follow channels). Those two different use cases had different navigation priorities — and resolving that tension became the central design problem for the Figma phase.

The analysis also surfaced an accessibility gap: hearing-impaired users in the original concept were assumed but not designed for. That became a design requirement, not an afterthought.

05

Iterate & Deliver

The Figma prototype implemented the findings directly — merged social/music navigation, higher contrast state indicators, and an explicit hearing-accessibility mode. The visual design direction (high contrast, vibrant but not overwhelming) came from the data too: festival environments are visually noisy, and the app needed to be readable in bad lighting.

The final high-fidelity prototype was presented to stakeholders in a combined display and video walkthrough — demonstrating both the visual design and the interactive flow end-to-end.

Project Implementation

Design Strategy

Feature prioritisation was driven by the research findings, not assumption. Social and discovery features came first; commerce (in-app purchases, collectibles) was scoped as secondary — present but not dominant — because the data showed users came for connection, not shopping.

Core features: Scrollable channel feed, friend location sharing, virtual dance party joining, festival map

Reducing Cognitive Load

At a festival, your phone competes with everything around you. The design uses progressive disclosure heavily — content appears when the user actively requests it, not upfront. Fewer decisions per screen, clearer information hierarchy, and touchscreen-first interaction patterns all reduce the mental effort of using the app in a distracting environment.

Principle: Show only what's needed in the moment; trust the user to navigate deeper when they want to

Inclusive Design

A silent disco app that doesn't work for hearing-impaired users is a design failure at the concept level. Accessibility wasn't retrofitted — once the data surfaced it as a gap, it became a requirement. Visual channel indicators, vibration cues, and captioning options were integrated into the core interaction model.

Considerations: Hearing accessibility, high-contrast visual modes, simplified language, contextual help
